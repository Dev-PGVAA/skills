# Design fundamentals — elements and relationships

Attribution retained from the source kit: adapted from BCcampus, *Graphic Design and Print Production Fundamentals* (CC BY 4.0), chapters 2–4. The guidance below is contextual craft practice, not a pass/fail standard.

## Visual elements

Lines, shapes, texture, color, imagery, and negative space should contribute to the subject and task. A rule can separate or connect; texture can evoke a material but reduce legibility; empty space can group related content without a container. Use the least visual machinery that makes the relationship clear.

## Principles in context

- **Contrast:** make consequential differences perceptible. Changing every attribute creates noise; priorities need relative weight.
- **Repetition:** recurring roles should feel consistent across screens, pages, and states. This does not require unrelated content to look identical.
- **Alignment:** a clear axis, grid, or optical relationship makes composition legible. Deliberate overlap and asymmetry can serve expressive work.
- **Proximity:** nearby elements are often read as related. Balance spacing with labels, borders, and common regions when relationships are more complex.
- **Balance:** distribute visual weight deliberately; symmetry can be calm, asymmetry dynamic. Neither is universally better.
- **Emphasis:** guide attention to the current task or key content. Complex operational views may have several focal regions.
- **White space:** tune to comprehension and density. Adding space is not a reason to remove required data or force excessive scrolling.
- **Unity and variety:** enough consistency to orient the reader, enough meaningful distinction to avoid flattening content.

## Gestalt grouping cues

| Cue | Useful application | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Proximity | Show fields or content belonging together | Dense layouts can need additional labels/boundaries |
| Similarity | Make recurring roles recognizable | Similar style can imply a false relationship |
| Closure | Cropped or partial visual motifs | Missing control boundaries can appear broken |
| Continuity | Aligned reading paths and connected flows | Accidental lines can suggest wrong order |
| Figure/ground | Separate active surface from background | Overlays and low contrast can obscure priority |
| Common region | Group cards, panels, or sections | Too many nested boxes create ambiguity |
| Connectedness | Show relationships with edges/arrows | Lines must match actual data or flow direction |

## Diagnose before decorating

When a surface feels cluttered, inspect competing roles, labels, grouping, and content relevance. When it feels flat, increase a meaningful distinction rather than adding decoration. Test perception on the actual artifact; heuristics cannot replace user tasks or observation.
